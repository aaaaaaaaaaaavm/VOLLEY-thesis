\title{VOLLEY Gen5: A System-Level Design Study of a Programmable Electromagnetic CubeSat Deployer}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Adityavardhan Mishra}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Department of Mechanical Engineering, Symbiosis Institute of Technology\\
Symbiosis International (Deemed University), Pune, India\\
PRN 23070125054}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Rideshare CubeSats commonly inherit the host orbit, while conventional spring deployers provide only a small release impulse. This paper reports Gen5, a fixed system-level computational design for a magazine-fed electromagnetic deployer intended to command the release velocity of unmodified 3U satellites. An ironless double-sided Halbach linear synchronous motor drives a reusable 9.445~kg sled to a 1.5~m release station on 1.8~m longerons; two cassettes hold twelve spacecraft and an eddy-current brake arrests the sled after release. Coupled electromagnetic, circuit, dynamics and mass models predict a 16.029~m/s exit velocity at 10.07~$g$, 2.78~kJ gross energy draw per shot and 126.6~kg dry mass. Closed-loop simulation predicts 0.0274~m/s ($3\sigma$) exit-velocity dispersion. For a stated 450~km, mean-solar-activity case, the orbital model predicts a 28.8~km semi-major-axis increase and a 1.60 lifetime multiplier after one prograde shot; the current rated lifetime result has not been independently rerun. A separate Cartesian propagator confirms the immediate 28.8~km two-body axis change, while a FreeCAD reference assembly exposes an 11~mm side-fed width shortfall and exact track--cassette interference. Independent checks cover selected model quantities, but full-depth thrust, payload compatibility, release-contact dynamics and host integration remain unverified. The design fails its own mass-per-satellite criterion: 10.547~kg versus approximately 2~kg. No article has been built, fired, measured, qualified or flown. Gen6 scaling toward a 1~km/s-class release is future research with no selected architecture.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Rideshare secondary payloads may be released into an orbit selected by the primary mission rather than their own target \cite{soa}. Conventional spring dispensers supply a small, payload-dependent separation impulse; flight-proven examples include J-SSOD and commercial CubeSat deployers \cite{jssod,exopod}. An operator needing different delivered states can also use host manoeuvres, onboard propulsion, differential drag or an orbital-transport service. A useful deployer comparison must hold payloads, starting orbit, final-state tolerances, deadline, integration burden and failure consequences constant. Repeatable small-satellite fleets are a plausible mission context, but no customer, payload or host has endorsed the Gen5 requirements in this paper.

Electromagnetic release is established prior art, not a novelty claim in itself. Feng \emph{et al.} model a high-acceleration induction launcher for a 20~kg CubeSat \cite{feng2025}; other work studies stacked-spacecraft transfer and reports a transport-mechanism prototype \cite{stacked2022,xu2024,storage2025}. The question investigated here is narrower: can a shared host-mounted device impart a modelled, individually commanded impulse to an unmodified 3U payload while meeting a chosen acceleration limit and system-level mass, power, containment and mission criteria? The 25~$g$ ceiling is a design choice, not a universal CubeSat qualification limit; the CubeSat Design Specification defers acceptance to the mission provider \cite{cds}.

This paper evaluates one fixed Gen5 computational configuration. It contributes a modeled machine and shot, selected field and circuit cross-checks, a mass and orbit trade, a reference assembly with an explicit packaging failure, and a register of conditions that prevent a product or flight claim. Host examples are reference calculations, not approved interfaces. The current results include negative design findings rather than a demonstrated customer benefit.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Deployment heritage}
The P-POD established the rail-and-spring standard, and the ISS-based J-SSOD and NanoRacks systems industrialized it \cite{jssod,soa}. The failure record is instructive: the NanoRacks ball-lock anomaly, in which jack-screw preload above 0.11~N$\cdot$m drove the release mechanism toward seizure, traced to a load path in which ascent preload passed through the release device itself \cite{lewis}. Zhao \emph{et al.} built a multi-CubeSat electromagnetic transport-and-ejection prototype and report selectable separation speeds of 0.4--0.85~m/s for 3U and 0.67--1.42~m/s for 1U payloads \cite{stacked2022}. Programmable electromagnetic release and stacked feed are therefore prior art. Gen5 instead predicts a 16.029~m/s 3U rated shot within a different modeled installed-system design; it has no measured shot and fails its own 3U mass and side-fed packaging criteria.

\subsection{Electromagnetic launch and supporting technology}
Coilgun research examined nanosatellite launch at km/s scales \cite{turman,kaye}, establishing contactless acceleration and its costs: the single-stage reluctance machines of that lineage convert 1--2\% of stored energy into kinetic energy, though multi-stage induction designs do far better, and the on-orbit CubeSat launcher of \cite{feng2025} reports 14.9--19.9\%. Ironless permanent-magnet linear machines occupy the opposite corner of the design space: the Halbach array concentrates flux on one working face \cite{halbach}, and the Inductrack program demonstrated large forces from such arrays with no iron and no sliding contact \cite{inductrack}. Finite Halbach arrays can introduce end-effect thrust ripple \cite{yamaguchi2023}, motivating a position-dependent 3-D force check for Gen5 rather than an imported correction factor. Space-oriented supercapacitor studies and component options are reported in ESA-led programmes \cite{faure,espc}, but no installed Gen5 cell string is qualified. Eddy-current damping has aerospace precedent \cite{eddy}; the proposed VOLLEY brake has not been tested. On the astrodynamics side, Planet Labs demonstrated propulsion-free constellation phasing by differential drag over campaigns of weeks to months \cite{foster,foster2}; that timescale is the benchmark against which impulsive seeding is measured. ISRO's POEM programme converted the spent PSLV fourth stage into a stabilized hosted-payload platform across four missions, closing POEM-3 with a controlled, debris-free reentry \cite{poem3,poem4}, providing a hosted-stage precedent, not regulatory approval for VOLLEY.

\section{System Architecture}
\subsection{Requirements}
The driving requirements are: eject an unmodified 3U CubeSat, imposing no armature, plating, or electrical interface on the customer satellite; hold payload acceleration below a \emph{chosen} design ceiling of 25~$g$, a requirement this work sets on itself rather than one any standard imposes; mount on a restartable upper stage, kick stage, or hosted orbital platform through a standard ESPA bolt pattern, a host class whose accommodation envelope is not public and against which the closed 1839~mm length therefore cannot yet be shown to fit (Sec.~\ref{sec:lim}); provide per-satellite programmable velocity with dispersion small against its astrodynamic effect; carry a twelve-satellite manifest with autonomous sequencing; provide inhibits and a defined abort; and hand recoil to the host in a form its attitude control absorbs at negligible propellant cost.

\subsection{Derivation of Principal Design Parameters}
The values that define the machine combine internal requirements, assumptions and externally sourced limits; Table~\ref{tab:derive} records the governing relation for each. Three deserve explicit statement. The acceleration ceiling is \textbf{chosen, not derived}. An earlier version of this work obtained it by integrating the NASA GEVS \cite{gevs} protoflight random-vibration spectrum to 14.1~$g_{\mathrm{rms}}$, taking a $3\sigma$ envelope of roughly 42~$g$ as the launch load, and setting a 25~$g$ cap beneath it. \textbf{That derivation is withdrawn.} Broadband random vibration and a 162~ms commanded acceleration are different load cases: $g_{\mathrm{rms}}$ is the root-mean-square of a stationary random process over a spectrum, and multiplying it by three produces a peak-response estimate for that process, not a quasi-static limit load a structure is qualified to carry. Neither GEVS nor the CubeSat Design Specification \cite{cds} publishes a universal quasi-static level for CubeSats; the CDS defers test levels to the launch provider, and levels therefore vary by vehicle and by mission. \textbf{25~$g$ is retained here as an internal design requirement} --- the number the retention chain, the cradle, the abort logic and the payload-family table are all sized against --- and it is not offered as evidence that any satellite is qualified for it. An earlier version of this design was acceleration-limited: at a 4.9~kg parametric sled the ejection ran at 16.3~$g$, close enough to the cap that the cap set the velocity through $v_{\max}=\sqrt{2a_{\max}L}$. That is no longer the binding constraint. With the sled computed at 9.4~kg from the CAD solid volumes the same commanded force accelerates a heavier moving mass, so the ejection sits at 10.1~$g$ and the 1.3~m zone yields 16.0~m/s. The machine is now \emph{thrust-and-mass} limited rather than acceleration-limited, with a factor of 2.5 of margin against its own ceiling left unused. Recovering velocity therefore means removing sled mass or raising sheet current, not shortening the stroke; a 0.2~m coast-trim zone completes the 1.5~m axis. Stroke buys velocity only as $\sqrt{L}$, so the trade is unattractive: total track lengths of 1.0, 1.2, 1.5 and 1.8~m give 12.6, 14.1, 16.0 and 17.8~m/s respectively, and the envelope is already the binding packaging constraint (Sec.~\ref{sec:lim}). The capacitor bank follows from the shot energy and the droop it actually reaches: at the 2.78~kJ draw the selected 6~F bank sags to $V_1=0.947V_0$, and
\begin{equation}
C=\frac{2E}{V_0^2-V_1^2}=6.00~\mathrm{F}\rightarrow 6~\mathrm{F},
\end{equation}
realized as thirty-two series 190~F, 3~V commercial cells for a 96~V rail compatible with 1200~V SiC devices at a comfortable derating.

\subsection{Topology Trade}
The payload settles the trade before the electromagnetics do. Exit velocity is bounded by
\begin{equation}
v_{\max}=\sqrt{2\,a_{\max}L},
\end{equation}
so at the 25~$g$ design ceiling over a 1.3--2~m stroke the reachable range is 25--31~m/s. The coilgun's defining advantage, a km/s-class ceiling, is outside this Gen5 payload load envelope, while its costs remain: microsecond timing against the suck-back effect, an armature bolted to the customer's satellite, and no abort once fired. Efficiency is deliberately not among them: at 14.9--19.9\% \cite{feng2025} a multi-stage coilgun is comparable to the figure reported here, and an earlier version of this argument claimed otherwise on the strength of a single-stage number. A linear synchronous motor concedes nothing in the reachable envelope and inverts every cost: high drive efficiency, continuous servo control, and a reusable sled that carries the magnets so the satellite carries nothing. \subsection{Layout and Firing Cycle}
The model places the release station 1.5~m from the breech on 1.8~m structural longerons; the powered stroke is 1.3~m, followed by a 0.2~m coast-and-trim interval (Figs.~\ref{fig:block} and \ref{fig:layout}). The stator is a double-sided ironless three-phase winding. The modeled mover is a 9.445~kg sled carrying opposed Halbach arrays (48~mm wavelength, 8~mm N45SH blocks, four blocks per wavelength) across a 12~mm winding gap. The two six-satellite cassettes are conceptual feed elements. Their current side-fed reference placement fails the width and exact-solid interference checks reported in Sec.~\ref{sec:lim}.

The intended sequence is follower advance, restraint engagement, motor acceleration, sled braking and payload departure. Feed forces, contact transitions, retention during ascent, release timing and tip-off have not been verified for an actual satellite. The current model assumes that the payload remains seated against the aft backstop during acceleration and separates when the sled decelerates; that assumption requires moving-contact analysis and instrumented tests. The ideal internal mass-motion model gives no final rigid-body rate after the sled stops, but it predicts a transient return rate of 0.130~deg/s for the assumed 500~kg host and 0.385~deg/s at 200~kg; both declared peak-rate bands fail. The earlier 8.2~s rate-null and 18.1~s cadence floor are superseded. An assumed 166~mm lever arm gives a 0.40~deg attitude offset in the 500~kg example. Controller restoration time and structural ringing have not been established. The adopted mission-planning interval is 1200~s between shots; 10--20~s is only a modeled recharge floor, so twelve scheduled shots span approximately 4.0~h and 2.6 orbits if all other operations succeed.

\subsection{Restraint, Inhibits, and Abort}
Launch restraint separates the preload path from the release path, the inverse of the NanoRacks fault \cite{lewis}. One-shot retention gates at each cassette exit carry ascent preload directly into structure; the escapement is caged during ascent; the sled is held by a motorized over-center cam lock. Firing requires a three-inhibit chain: redundant seat switches, an attitude-valid flag from the host, and a sequencer arm. Abort is available to approximately 45\% of the stroke, beyond which braking distance for the combined mass exceeds remaining track; the muzzle buffer is sized to capture an aborted combined mass. The retention gate carries the ascent preload of a six-satellite stack through two D9 A-286 shear pins of 41.0~kN double-shear capacity; the escapement, caged behind the gate, sees none of this. The quasi-static case taken at this design's own 25~$g$ ceiling, $24\,\mathrm{kg}\times25\,g=5.9$~kN, is \emph{not} the sizing case, and the gate was originally sized against it with two D6 pins. Miles' equation on the GEVS protoflight spectrum (14.1~$g_\mathrm{rms}$, 0.16~$g^2$/Hz) at the track's 109~Hz fixed-fixed mode gives a 3$\sigma$ load of 11.7~kN at $Q=10$ and 20.2~kN at $Q=30$, two to three and a half times the quasi-static figure, which left the D6 margin negative at $Q=30$. The pins were not undersized; the load case was. Resizing to D9 raises the margin of safety to $+0.45$ at $Q=30$ and keeps it positive across $Q=10$--$30$ for an added mass of 11~g, so the retention chain no longer depends on where the unmeasured structural damping lands---which matters because the same unmeasured $Q$ governs the force-ripple resonance discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:lim} and cannot be resized away there. Random vibration, not quasi-static preload, is the driving case for the retention chain, and is now the case it is sized against. The abort threshold is where the sled braking distance under the same thrust equals the remaining track, which for the loaded 13.4~kg carriage falls at 45\% of stroke and 11.1~m/s. The commit fraction is unchanged by the heavier sled---braking distance and travelled distance scale with mass identically, so the mass cancels---but the velocity at the commit point falls with the acceleration.

\subsection{Arrest, Power, and Materials}
The sled leaves release with about 1.21~kJ. Motor regeneration alone cannot arrest it, because braking force is bounded by the same thrust constant as acceleration; that bound sets what regeneration can and cannot do, and both halves matter. Braking at the rated sheet current over the 39~mm of stator the closed envelope allows downstream of release extracts 51.8~J of mechanical work for 0.4~J of copper, returning \textbf{47~J} to the bank, and the sled still reaches the brake at 15.7~m/s carrying \textbf{1162~J}. A copper-fin eddy-current brake therefore provides bulk arrest: force proportional to velocity, contactless, with flight heritage in the damper class \cite{eddy}. Authority is abundant, so the pole entry is tapered to cap sled deceleration near 200~$g$. This limit is set by the magnet retention: a 65~g Halbach block at 200~$g$ loads its bonded interface (10.8~cm$^2$) to only 0.12~MPa against a conservative 10~MPa epoxy allowable, a margin of 84, so 200~$g$ is a deliberate conservatism protecting the brittle sinter rather than a strength limit. The 0.344~kg copper fin absorbs the sled's residual 1162~J per shot at an adiabatic 9.0~K per-shot transient rise; cooling between shots has not been modelled; a ring-spring catches the residual below 1.5~m/s (Fig.~\ref{fig:brake}). Pulse energy comes from a 6~F, 96~V supercapacitor bank switched by a SiC bridge \cite{faure}. Materials follow three rules: nothing conductive rides the changing field (titanium sled chassis, PEEK formers), nothing near the track is ferromagnetic except by design (BeCu springs, A-286 fasteners), and nothing outgasses near customer optics (E595-compliant polymers, dry films, sealed roller cartridges).

\section{Models and Verification}
\subsection{Field Model and Independent Verification}
The Halbach airgap field is a decaying wave, $B(y)=B_0e^{-ky}$ with $k=2\pi/\lambda$, summed over the two opposed faces. Because this carries the force budget, it was checked against an independent magnetostatic computation (analytic cuboid superposition, magpylib), with the array rotation-sense convention determined empirically by probing a single array on both faces; the exercise caught two successive sign errors whose outputs matched single-array fingerprints. Converged results: single-array mid-gap 0.351~T against the wave model's 0.351~T; double-sided mid-gap peak 0.694~T against a predicted 0.702~T. The stray field behind the array back face, which sets the magnetic keep-out for stored satellites, computes to 22.7~mT at 10~mm, 4.3~mT at 20~mm, and 0.4~mT at 50~mm.

\subsection{Winding-Resolved Thrust Constant}
The airgap field is sampled in all three dimensions before the Lorentz sum: $B_y$ is Gauss-Legendre averaged across the array's full 90~mm depth rather than taken on the centre plane. That distinction is worth stating because the centre-plane value is 4.4\% higher, and using it---as this work did until an independent depth-resolved integral was run---overstates the thrust constant by the same margin and the exit velocity by 2.2\%.
A slotless three-phase belt winding (pole pitch $\lambda/2$, belts of $\lambda/6$, winding factor 0.955) was resolved against the verified field by direct Lorentz integration with position-locked sinusoidal commutation, the field translated with the sled. The result is a thrust constant
\begin{equation}
K_t = 10.54~\mathrm{N/(kA/m)},\quad \text{ripple } \pm1.01\%\ (6\text{th harm.}),
\end{equation}
shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ripple}. Fig.~\ref{fig:fieldmap} shows the field the integral is taken over, and the reason the integral must be taken over it: sampled on the array's centre plane $B_y$ peaks at \textbf{0.5041~T}, while the mean across the 90~mm depth is \textbf{0.4759~T}, a ratio of \textbf{0.9440}. Treating the centre-plane value as though it held across the depth is the 4.42\% overstatement Sec.~\ref{sec:lim} records.

\subsection{Shot, Servo, and Astrodynamic Models}
The shot integrates sled-plus-satellite dynamics against the supercapacitor circuit with explicit copper loss and a 95\% converter. Velocity control uses a position-scheduled profile with proportional correction and a coast-trim residual correction from photogate measurement; an 800-run Monte Carlo over thrust-constant, mass, force-ripple, and sensor tolerances gives the dispersion. The proportional gain is not tuned to that dispersion but designed against stability margins, as Sec.~\ref{sec:control} sets out. Orbital lifetime uses per-revolution orbit-averaged decay (Gauss tangential equations, quadrature over eccentric anomaly, static exponential atmosphere at mean activity \cite{vallado}); the method was cross-validated against an independent Cowell RK4 propagation with drag, the two agreeing on 30-day semi-major-axis decay to 99.4\%. Constellation seeding uses $\Delta a = 2a\,\Delta v/v$ and the resulting period difference; deployment safety propagates the stage and all deployed ellipses with Kepler-plus-secular-$J_2$ motion for 30 days, with candidate minima refined at 0.25~s sampling.

\subsection{Velocity-Loop Stability}\label{sec:control}
The control law divides the commanded sheet current by the modelled thrust constant and multiplies by the modelled mass, so the plant's own $K_t/m$ cancels and the open-loop transfer reduces to $L(s)=K_p\,e^{-s\tau}/s$. The proportional gain is therefore not a current gain but an acceleration per unit velocity error, in $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, and \emph{its numeric value is the gain crossover frequency in rad/s}. The total lag $\tau$ is the sensing and computation transport delay plus the half-sample of a zero-order hold; both are pure delays and add. No sensor has been selected, so the delay is a stated assumption, swept rather than asserted, and 0.6~ms is used as the reference value with a 0.1~ms hold at 5~kHz.

That reduction sets the design constraint. An earlier gain of 3500~$\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ places the crossover at 557~Hz---five times the track's 109~Hz first bending mode and above the 48~Hz mode as well---giving $-50.4^\circ$ of phase margin and $-3.9$~dB of gain margin at the reference delay, with marginal stability reached at only 0.45~ms of total lag (Figs.~\ref{fig:bode} and \ref{fig:lat}). Two properties of the simulation had concealed this: the loop fed back an undelayed state, which is the one condition under which that gain is stable, and the command is clipped at the winding rating, so the linearly unstable loop behaves as a bang-bang relay whose mean follows the feedforward term while the terminal photogate trim removes the residual. The reported dispersion was consequently governed by the saturation limits and the terminal correction rather than by the feedback it was attributed to, which is why the closed-loop figure is reported here only alongside the margins that make it a property of a design.

The gain is instead sized as the largest value simultaneously holding $\geq 50^\circ$ of phase margin at the reference delay and keeping closed-loop bandwidth at or below one third of the first bending mode, which gives 195~$\mathrm{s^{-1}}$: crossover 31.1~Hz, phase margin $+82.2^\circ$, gain margin $+21.2$~dB, and no excursion above the current rating anywhere in the stroke. \textbf{The gain falls by a factor of eighteen and the $3\sigma$ dispersion does not move}, 0.0271 to 0.0267~m/s. The loop never required the bandwidth: dispersion is set by the terminal trim and by the thrust-constant and mass tolerances, not by loop gain. The binding constraint is now the structural one, so a measured first mode below 109~Hz would lower the designed gain with it. Classical margins on a 162~ms trajectory are necessary rather than sufficient, the plant is treated as rigid, and the structural modes enter only as a frequency the bandwidth is held away from; a compliant-track model remains outstanding.

\section{Performance Results}
\subsection{Launch Performance}
At the rated point (3U reference, 9.445~kg sled input from historical Gen3 CAD solid volumes, 1.3~m acceleration): exit velocity \textbf{16.0~m/s} at \textbf{10.1~g}, pulse 162~ms, peak current 320~A, and bank state-of-charge sag 5.2\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:shot}). Energy drawn is 2.78~kJ per shot, with copper heat of 854~J and a further 76~J dissipated in the bank's own series resistance. Regenerative braking returns 47~J after release, so the net draw is 2.74~kJ. The payload receives 514~J, a \textbf{18.8\% electrical-to-payload efficiency}; the sled's share is split between 47~J recovered and 1162~J dissipated in the arrest brake. Efficiency falls with the heavier sled for two compounding reasons: a larger fraction of the same mechanical work goes into a mass that is then braked away, and the longer 162~ms pulse accrues more copper loss at unchanged current density. Recovery is what partially offsets the first of those, and it is bounded by geometry rather than by loss: over the available 39~mm the braking pulse lasts 2.5~ms and burns 0.4~J of copper, against 854~J over the 162~ms shot, so recovered energy rises monotonically with braking force up to the winding's rating and with distance beyond it. A 500~mm section would return 540~J and a 1~m section would arrest the sled outright while returning 727~J, but the closed envelope already exceeds its target class by 44\% (Sec.~\ref{sec:lim}), so distance is not available here. Peak current during recovery is 212~A, below the shot's own 320~A, so the drive is not re-rated. The closed-loop Monte Carlo returns a dispersion of \textbf{0.0274~m/s (3$\sigma$)} at a 15.8~m/s fleet setpoint (Fig.~\ref{fig:mc}), dominated by the sensing chain rather than plant tolerances. The setpoint must sit below the open-loop ceiling for the servo to retain authority; at 98.85\% of it, the nominal headroom is 0.188~m/s. Table~\ref{tab:family} gives the payload family: the system is force-limited above 1U, so heavier payloads trade velocity, never structural margin, and the deployer mass each customer pays for varies by a factor of 109 across the family (Sec.~\ref{sec:family}).

A separate algebraic audit in the companion repository recalculates the payload kinetic energy, constant-acceleration equivalent stroke, dry-mass sum and brake-work identities from the captured JSON. These agree within the declared rounding bands. Subtracting the reported payload and sled kinetic energies, shot copper heat and bank ESR from gross electrical draw leaves 124.488~J (4.47\%) unitemized. This is an accounting gap, not an identified physical loss; a complete electrical budget and supplier-backed installed bank remain open.

\subsection{Payload Class and the Mass-per-Satellite Argument}\label{sec:family}
Table~\ref{tab:family} is read most usefully from its last column rather than its first. Exit velocity is nearly flat across the family, because the 9.445~kg sled is most of the moving mass: removing the payload entirely buys 19\% of velocity, so velocity is a property of the sled and the stroke and not of the customer. Shortening the magnet array does not recover it either, since array length sets thrust and sled mass together---a 150~mm array gives a 5.15~kg sled but a 4.65~N per kA/m thrust constant, and the 3U case falls to 12.9~m/s.

The column that does move is deployer mass per satellite: by a factor of 27 between the 3U case and PocketQube~1P, and by 109 across the full column. At 3U the machine spends \textbf{10.55~kg of deployer per customer}, which loses to a cold-gas propulsion module delivering the same 16.0~m/s at 0.5--1.2~kg by a factor of between nine and twenty-one---12.4 against the 0.85~kg mid-class figure. At PocketQube~1P it spends \textbf{0.388~kg} and wins by 1.3 to 3.1. The comparison inverts on payload class, and on no machine parameter at all: not the thrust constant, not the stroke, not the sheet current. It inverts less decisively than it did: the figures published until 2026-08-20 were 7.04 and 0.24~kg, and the enclosure buildup that replaced an 8.00~kg packaging placeholder with 50.04~kg of derived line items moved both.

Three qualifications belong with those counts rather than beneath them. First, they are volumetric. The packing efficiency is calibrated against the one configuration that has been laid out in CAD---the 3U case, where a raw volume ratio predicts 21 and the magazine holds 12---and the resulting 56\% is then applied to classes for which no cassette exists. Second, the feed engages the CubeSat Design Specification corner-rail interface at the cradle, the escapement and the retention gate; PocketQubes, ThinSats and TubeSats do not have that interface, so a smaller class needs its own cassette, cradle and gate, which is mechanical design rather than a parameter change. Third, hundreds of satellites per load is a different machine from twelve: the campaign thermal case, the bank recharge duty and the escapement cycle life were all sized for twelve shots, and the bank is already the binding constraint at that number (Sec.~\ref{sec:lim}). What Table~\ref{tab:family} establishes is that payload class dominates the economic argument, not that a small-payload variant has been designed.

The comparison the 3U column loses most clearly is against the incumbent it was meant to match. A canisterised spring dispenser occupies roughly 6~kg per 3U slot, and an acceptance band written before the comparison script existed asked for parity within 15\%. The measured ratio is \textbf{1.758}: this deployer is 76\% heavier per satellite than the hardware it would displace, and the band fails. It failed on a change to this work rather than to the comparator---a packaging rollup that had carried an 8.00~kg placeholder was replaced by 50.04~kg of derived line items, and parity did not survive it. The project's own kill criterion on mass per satellite, fixed at an estimated 2~kg, is crossed by \textbf{5.3$\times$}. No enclosure change reaches parity: the lightest alternative structure costed, a honeycomb sandwich, would give 8.87~kg per satellite and a ratio of 1.48. The route that moves this criterion is a smaller payload class, which is what the left of Table~\ref{tab:family} prices and what Sec.~\ref{sec:lim} records has not been designed.

It is not for want of looking. Every kilogram of the dry mass was attributed to the requirement that causes it---six requirements, from \emph{the satellite is unmodified} to \emph{twelve satellites share one drive}---and each of the 64 subsets was then deleted in turn to find the lightest machine any combination of relaxations could reach (Fig.~\ref{fig:ledger}). Three of the six carry no mass on their own. The best corner deletes both of the two that do, giving up the reusable mover and the requirement that the energy arrive during the shot, and it removes \textbf{37.89~kg, or 29.90\%}. \textbf{88.67~kg survives every deletion in all 64 corners}---70.06\% of dry mass, \textbf{7.39~kg per satellite against a 2~kg criterion}. Deleting requirements does not close it; only carrying more, smaller satellites does. The ledger is an attribution and an upper bound on what relaxation buys, not a sizing model, and it does not claim the surviving mass is irreducible by better design.

The architecture does not meet the spring-dispenser mass parity criterion. \subsection{Astrodynamic Utility}\label{sec:astro}
A prograde ejection raises apogee while leaving perigee at the deployment altitude, so the correct claim is a longer-lived ellipse. At 450~km with ballistic coefficient 61~kg/m$^2$, one 16.0~m/s shot multiplies lifetime by \textbf{1.60} at mean solar activity (base lifetimes 0.52--2.61~yr across the activity range; Figs.~\ref{fig:life} and \ref{fig:uq}).

An earlier version of this work claimed the multiplier was invariant to two decimal places across ballistic coefficients of 40--90~kg/m$^2$ and across a fivefold density range, and offered that invariance as the result the analysis could defend. That claim does not survive an independent propagator. Run headless against MSISE90 with 20$\times$20 gravity, lunisolar third bodies and solar radiation pressure, GMAT R2022a reproduces the multiplier at mean and high activity but returns 2.07 at low activity, a spread of 18\% against a 5\% acceptance band fixed before the run. The reason is a defect in how the sweep was posed rather than in the arithmetic: the model represents solar activity as a uniform multiplicative scale on density, and ballistic coefficient enters the drag term in the same multiplicative slot, so both sweeps divide the two lifetimes being compared by the same factor. Their ratio cannot move, whatever the atmosphere does. A real atmosphere changes the \emph{shape} of the density--altitude profile with activity, the boosted orbit's apogee samples that profile some 37~km higher than the baseline's, and the ratio then does move. The multiplier is therefore quoted here at a stated activity level, and no invariance is claimed.

Differential ejection velocities put adjacent satellites on different periods: splits of 2, 5, and 10~m/s reach 30$^\circ$ spacing in \textbf{6.9, 2.8, and 1.4 days}. Under identical density and geometry assumptions, differential drag between 3:1 area configurations achieves the same spacing in \textbf{25.0 days} (Fig.~\ref{fig:dragvs}), consistent with flown drag-phasing campaigns operating over weeks to months \cite{foster}.

A common-epoch comparison must account for the actual state imparted at release. In a two-body reference case, a satellite released with zero relative velocity from an unchanged host follows the host orbit. A second identical release later follows that same trajectory; at any common time the two satellites are co-located. The host's angular travel between release events is not persistent separation between the satellites. The 1200~s inter-shot cadence is therefore a thermal and recharge assumption, not a phasing result. Conventional spring impulses, host manoeuvres and subsequent differential drift remain valid comparators and must be included in any mission-level phasing trade.

A differential deployment impulse can change orbital period and create a growing phase separation, as the preceding model shows. It does not establish a permanent fixed angular offset without later control or drift management. The differential-drag comparison in Fig.~\ref{fig:dragvs} is conditional on the stated area and atmosphere assumptions. A matched comparison of commanded electromagnetic shots, spring deployment, host manoeuvres and drag is still needed before asserting a phasing advantage.

A current-rated cross-check independently propagates the Cartesian post-release state for two orbital periods and recovers a 28.800775~km semi-major-axis rise at 16.029~m/s. The 0.02~m solver/identity band passes. This verifies instantaneous two-body geometry, not the 1.60 lifetime multiplier, host campaign or launcher performance. The method and exact inputs are archived in the companion repository.

The Gen5 model instead evaluates a commanded change in the deployed satellite's orbit. Its 16.0~m/s prograde shot predicts a 28.8~km semi-major-axis increase and a 1.60 lifetime multiplier at the stated mean-activity condition. A 2.5~m/s spring is also an orbital impulse and produces an 8.2\% modelled lifetime extension under the same setup. Release timing without an impulse changes neither orbit; a manoeuvring host can change the initial orbit of later releases. These are different mission cases, and the quoted Gen5 benefit is model output rather than demonstrated performance.

\subsection{Deployment Safety}
Every prograde shot leaves an ellipse whose perigee sits at the firing altitude, so the fleet re-crosses the stage's orbit by construction, and the along-track phase between a deployed satellite and the stage realigns on a period of \textbf{10.3 days} at the rated velocity (Fig.~\ref{fig:conj}). The closest approach reached within a 30-day screening window is deliberately \emph{not} quoted here as a safety figure: because both bodies re-cross the same altitude, that minimum is fixed by the exact along-track phase at crossing, which is acutely sensitive to ejection velocity---in the model a $\pm2.5\%$ change about the rated point sweeps the 30-day minimum by more than an order of magnitude. It is a near-resonant sample of the beat geometry, not a design property. Two statements survive that sensitivity and carry the safety argument. First, per-shot collision-avoidance (COLA) screening is \textbf{mandatory} for every ejection, because the approach geometry cannot be certified from the nominal velocity alone. Second, disposing of the host stage before the first \mbox{10.3-day} realignment---routine for a restartable stage---removes the recurring close-approach mechanism at its source, before the fleet can return to the stage's along-track position. Per-shot conjunction products remain a mission-operations deliverable.

\subsection{Error, Tip-Off, and Mass}
The 0.0274~m/s dispersion maps to \textbf{$\pm$0.10~km apogee placement} at 450~km: sub-kilometre deterministic insertion for a satellite with no propulsion. Tip-off is modelled rather than bounded, and the risk is not where the analytic budget put it. Release occurs 200~mm---12.2~ms---into the coast-and-trim zone with commanded force already at zero, so at the residual latch and friction load the mechanism tolerates roughly 250~$\mu$s of asymmetry and still lands two orders of magnitude inside the 2~deg/s comparator. What is not benign is the start of the stroke: the payload centre of mass sits 70~mm off the thrust line, the 413~N push therefore applies a 28.9~N$\cdot$m moment, and the payload accelerates across its cradle clearance at 688~rad/s$^2$, arriving at 36--231~deg/s depending on fit---18 to 115 times the comparator. Whether that impact has damped out by release depends on a restitution model this study does not have, so tip-off is stated as a requirement on a cradle preload exceeding the 85~N couple reaction and on a release residual below about 1~N, against a release mechanism that remains undefined (Fig.~\ref{fig:tipoff}). Recoil per shot is the satellite's momentum only, \textbf{64.1~N$\cdot$s}, the sled's share returning through the brake. The solid model closes dry mass at \textbf{126.6~kg} (loaded 174.6~kg, centre of gravity 0.46~m from the breech), with the ironless stator at 7.3~kg of copper and formers; the system uses roughly a third of an ESPA Grande \emph{mass} allocation. It does not currently fit the corresponding envelope: the closed CAD assembly spans 1839~mm along the track against the roughly 1270~mm of that class, because the arrest brake sits beyond the release point and the enclosure must span it. The third of those routes is the one taken: the target host class is a restartable upper stage, kick stage, or hosted orbital platform, and ESPA-Grande port compliance is not a requirement of this design. That is a re-scope, not a fix, and its cost is stated rather than absorbed. Shortening the track to 1270~mm would leave a 731~mm acceleration zone and, since velocity goes as $\sqrt{L}$, would cut ejection from 16.0 to 12.0~m/s---a 25\% loss in the quantity every performance claim here rests on. The re-scope avoids that and gives up the ESPA-Grande port population in exchange. It also leaves the packaging question unanswerable for now rather than answered: no accommodation envelope for the target host class is public, so the 1839~mm length cannot presently be shown to fit anything, and obtaining that envelope is part of the same single data exchange Sec.~\ref{sec:host} identifies. This is an open item, and the honest description of it is that a measured failure against a published limit has become an unmeasured unknown against an undisclosed one.

\subsection{Integration Interfaces}
A candidate integration would need a provider-approved mechanical interface, an electrical recharge and inhibit interface, an authenticated command/telemetry interface, and verified attitude, thermal, magnetic, debris and disposal constraints. \subsection{Demonstration Path}
\subsection{What Air Costs a Ground Test}\label{sec:airdrag}
The machine operates in vacuum and every velocity above is computed with no aerodynamic term, but the full-scale demonstration fires a mass simulator down the track in a laboratory at sea level, so the comparison that test exists to make is between a measured velocity and a computed one that omits the air. A steady RANS solution ($k$-$\omega$ SST) around the sled and payload \emph{as generated}, meshed directly from the CAD surfaces rather than from an idealised body, gives a drag coefficient of \textbf{0.52} on a 201.6~cm$^2$ projected frontal area at $Re = 7.0\times10^5$, i.e. 1.73~N at exit velocity. Because the profile is position-scheduled, $v^2$ rises linearly with distance and so does the drag, making the work over the 1.30~m acceleration zone 1.13~J and the exit-velocity deficit \textbf{5.1~mm/s}, or 0.031~\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:airdrag}).

That figure is small against the design point and \emph{not} small against the quantity a ground test resolves: it is \textbf{19~\% of the 3$\sigma$ closed-loop dispersion}. An atmospheric ground test can be considered for initial velocity measurements, but its air-load correction and uncertainty must be reported; vacuum and thermal testing remain separate qualification questions. The distinction depends on how the test is run: open-loop, the deficit appears as 5.1~mm/s of velocity; closed-loop, the servo nulls it and it appears instead as 1.73~N of additional commanded force, 0.125~\% of the shot.

\section{Sensitivity and Uncertainty}\label{sec:sens}

\section{Mechanical Analysis}\label{sec:mech}
The structural load path is sized to the two governing cases: launch vibration during ascent and the arrest impulse during operation. Fastener and pin margins are quoted on ultimate strength at a 1.4 design factor per common launch-hardware practice, consistent with the qualification philosophy of GEVS \cite{gevs} and ECSS structural standards \cite{ecss}.

\emph{Track stiffness.} The dominant flight requirement is a first natural frequency above the launch-vehicle primary band, conventionally 70--100~Hz for secondary structure. Modeling the twin-longeron track as a uniform beam carrying the 20~kg stator distributed mass, the fundamental is $f_1=(\lambda_1^2/2\pi L^2)\sqrt{EI/\mu}$ with $EI$ from two 7075 box sections. A simply-supported idealization gives 48~Hz, but bracketing both ends into the ESPA ring (fixed-fixed) raises the first mode to 109~Hz, clearing the requirement; the design therefore specifies end-fixed mounting with an intermediate rib.

\emph{Inter-array attraction.} The two Halbach faces attract across the gap with a Maxwell stress $p=B^2/2\mu_0\approx120$~kPa at the 0.55~T mean working field, or 3.7~kN over the array footprint. Carried as a bending load into the titanium side plates, this produces 33~MPa against the 880~MPa Ti-6Al-4V yield, a margin above 20; the attraction is structurally minor and, being static, does not fatigue-cycle. The flat-plate expression overstates the force: a three-dimensional integration of the field gradient over the meshed blocks returns 2.69~kN, some 37\% lower, because the assumed 0.55~T is not the mean normal field over the separation plane. Jensen's inequality acts in the opposite direction and does not explain the overestimate. Extending the Maxwell-stress plane beyond the magnet footprint gives 2.680~kN, within 0.25\% of the 2.687~kN volume-force calculation; this is model-to-model agreement, not experimental validation. The earlier finite-element load remains conservative.

\emph{Arrest and roller loads.} At the 200~$g$ arrest cap the 9.445~kg sled develops 18.5~kN axially, reacted by the ring-spring stop; the pitch couple during braking loads the guide rollers to roughly 1.48~kN per pair. This is the load path most affected by adopting the CAD-derived sled mass---it very nearly doubles---and it is the one place where the heavier sled makes the machine harder rather than merely slower. Bearing selection at 1.48~kN per pair is within the rating of a 440C/Si$_3$N$_4$ hybrid of this size, but the reuse duty is not addressed here: the sled is braked twelve times per campaign in vacuum, and no lubrication or contact-life assessment has been performed. The abort latch, engaged only for a commanded abort, carries the 13.4~kg carriage at the abort deceleration, 0.42~kN, through dual A-286 pins at a margin above eight.

\emph{Fatigue and tolerance.} Operational cycling is benign: the magnet bond sees 0.12~MPa per shot against a 10~MPa allowable, far below any endurance concern over the few hundred shots of a service life. Positional accuracy is set by the winding-to-magnet gap, whose $-13\%$/mm thrust sensitivity dictates a $\pm0.05$~mm shim tolerance to hold thrust within $\pm0.65\%$; this is a standard precision-shim assembly, not a novel requirement.

\subsection{Architecture reliability, and the bar it sets}
A spring dispenser is twelve independent one-shot mechanisms in parallel: one failure costs one satellite. This design is one mechanism in series with itself, cycled twelve times. The sled, stator, converter, energy store, sequencer and brake serve every shot, and the escapement and retention gate cycle twelve times each, so a failure at shot $k$ forfeits shots $k$ through twelve. Enumerating the shot chain, \textbf{nine of thirteen elements forfeit the remaining manifest on a single failure, against zero for a spring dispenser}, and nine shared elements over twelve cycles is 108 opportunities to fail. This is structural and no component quality removes it.

What component quality does change is where the comparison lands. Writing $p$ for the probability that the shared chain survives one shot, expected delivery is $\sum_{k=1}^{12}p^k$, which departs from a spring's independent $12q$ quickly. Against a spring at $q=0.99$, matching on \emph{satellite count} requires $p=0.9985$, which is not a realistic target for a twelve-cycle electromechanical system without flight heritage. Matching on \emph{delivered orbital life} requires only $p=0.9347$, because each delivered satellite is worth 1.50 times a spring-deployed one. Expressed per element per cycle, the requirement is $r\geq0.99326$.

\textbf{That is the first quantitative reliability requirement this design has carried, and it is not yet met or measured}: no cycle-life test exists for the escapement, the gate or the sled. Two candidate mitigations are defined but are deliberately not credited above, because crediting them without analysis is the optimism the exercise exists to avoid. Retention gates are per cassette, so one gate fault forfeits six rather than twelve. And the winding is segmented: a dead segment is a length the sled coasts over rather than a stopped machine, so at four segments a failure downstream of the breech still exits at 14.19~m/s, 86.6\% of nominal. The exception is the breech segment itself, where no force acts on a stationary sled and the shot never starts, which makes duplicating or overlapping that one segment the cheapest available reliability action.

\section{Thermal Analysis}\label{sec:thermal}
The shot model assigns approximately 855~J to stator copper, 1162~J to sled braking, 93~J to the converter and 78~J to the bank ESR per rated cycle. Multiplying an assumed twelve-shot total of 26.6~kJ by a uniform 13.5~kJ/K structure thermal capacitance gives a 2.0~K \emph{bulk-average} rise; it says little about local peak temperatures. The corrected 0.344~kg fin mass gives a 9.0~K adiabatic rise per shot and a 108~K twelve-shot no-cooling bound. A transient conduction/radiation model has not established cooling between shots at the adopted 1200~s cadence. The 0.32~m$^2$ radiator estimate uses an assumed 130~W rejection case and is not a closed thermal design. Component survival temperatures, heater power and irreversible magnet loss require supplier data and orbit-specific hot/cold cases. The rated thermal claims remain model estimates, not environmental qualification.

\section{Power Electronics}\label{sec:pe}
The electrical architecture proposes a 96~V, thirty-two-cell pulse bank and a three-phase SiC inverter with position-synchronized control. The winding model predicts 19.7~$\mu$H, a 373~A peak phase current and 25.2~m$\Omega$ per phase at the rated point; 320~A is the reported DC-link peak and is a different quantity. At the assumed 20--40~kHz switching range, the calculated current ripple and copper contribution are model outputs. Device ratings, switching overshoot, gate-drive loss, cell balancing, fault-clearing energy and flight derating have not been checked with a selected part list or a physical converter.

Current limiting, bus clamping, desaturation shutdown, balancing, bleed/passivation, segmentation, shielding and dual sensing are proposed controls. None has a closed circuit schematic, thermal qualification, EMC measurement, fault-injection result or provider acceptance in this study. A shorted segment can halt a shot or create an unsafe transient; segmentation cannot yet be credited as fail-operational. The permanent-magnet field and inverter transients may reach other payloads, so scheduling after primary separation is not a substitute for payload-specific magnetic and EMC analysis.

\section{Space Environment, EMC, and Standards}\label{sec:env}
LEO accommodation would require a selected material and component list checked for outgassing, atomic oxygen, thermal cycling, radiation, vibration and shock against the actual provider and mission standards \cite{ecss}. The current model proposes low-outgassing polymers, protected surfaces and N45SH magnets, but no supplier qualification record, launch-load test or environmental test is supplied. The calculated 109~Hz first mode and selected load margins apply to stated structural idealizations; they do not demonstrate assembly survivability.

The permanent-magnet sled is a payload compatibility concern. The modelled back-face field is 22.7~mT at 10~mm, 4.3~mT at 20~mm and 0.4~mT at 50~mm for the stated geometry. Across a 3U payload envelope, the calculated field is 61.1~mT at the near face and 0.341~mT at the far face. The near value exceeds a representative $\pm$100~$\mu$T magnetometer full scale by 611 times; even the far value exceeds it by 3.4 times. These are model results, not a qualified payload field map. No published VOLLEY ICD defines a keep-out radius, and the proposed steel septa and shielding have not been evaluated as installed hardware. Magnetometer recovery, magnetorquer operation, permanent magnetisation of payload parts and switching-transient coupling must be assessed with a named satellite. Timing a firing after primary separation does not remove the static-field exposure of stored spacecraft.

\section{Manufacturing, Cost, and Scalability}\label{sec:mfg}
A possible fabrication sequence uses a wound copper stator on an insulating former, a restrained fixture for assembly of the magnet arrays, and late loading of spacecraft after mechanism inspection. Proposed slot and gap tolerances of $\pm0.1$ and $\pm0.05$~mm are design targets, not supplier capabilities or a completed stack-up. The current STEP shapes omit fasteners, harness, joints and feed motion, and the side-fed reference assembly clashes; therefore no released manufacturing drawing set, assembly process or recurring maintenance interval can be claimed. An eventual ground article would require measured current-to-velocity calibration and acceptance of the complete release cycle.

The cost model is parametric and has no supplier quotations. In its assumed bill of materials, avionics, pulse storage and sensing dominate the estimated recurring hardware cost more than the magnet set; this ordering is conditional on unquoted prices and the incomplete installed system. The rated shot draws 2.78~kJ, less than 1~Wh of electrical energy, but that is not a service-cost comparison: host power, storage, integration, qualification, mass, reliability, operations and any host propellant must be counted. A matched mission comparison with qualified spring systems, host manoeuvres, onboard propulsion and transport services remains open.

Ideal scaling trends ($v\propto\sqrt{L}$ at fixed acceleration) motivate a wider trade, but the payload-family table is model output, not accommodation of other CubeSat sizes. More payloads change feeder geometry, structure, reliability and host burden; the current twelve-slot reference already fails a width check. Lunar or deep-space use would require new interfaces, thermal/radiation design, mission dynamics and payload qualification, and is outside this study.

\section{Operational Concept and Maturation}\label{sec:conops}
The proposed operations sequence is launch with the deployer dormant; host and payload health checks; bank charge inside a provider-authorized window; one commanded release followed by independent confirmation of separation and safe sled arrest; and repetition only after fault review. Inhibit logic, an early-stroke abort window, bank passivation and eventual host disposal are design requirements, not demonstrated functions. POEM-3 provides a hosted-stage precedent \cite{poem3}, but no POEM or other provider has approved this sequence for VOLLEY.

The next evidence sequence is a reviewed feeder redesign, component-level bench tests, an instrumented integrated ground article, environmental tests against a named payload and host, and only then a provider-approved captive or deployment demonstration. Each stage needs predeclared acceptance bands and a decision review. No technology-readiness level is assigned from the present numerical results, and a future test does not automatically confer one.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:lim}
The new 20-instance FreeCAD reference assembly is reviewable as native FCStd and STEP, but its as-drawn side-fed placement fails the 11~mm width screen and has exact track--cassette overlap (Fig.~\ref{fig:packaging}). It is not a solved feeder, tolerance stack or manufacturing release. Eight source solids are CadQuery-generated and re-exported through FreeCAD; their native FreeCAD instances do not carry parametric feature histories. Seven limits bound the claims. \textbf{The first is that payload structural compatibility is not established, and cannot be established by this work alone.} The 25~$g$ ceiling this machine is designed to is a requirement it sets on itself. It was previously presented as following from the CubeSat qualification environment, by integrating a GEVS random-vibration spectrum to 14.1~$g_{\mathrm{rms}}$ and taking three times that as a bounding load; \textbf{that reasoning is withdrawn}, because a root-mean-square level over a broadband random spectrum is not a quasi-static limit load and a $3\sigma$ multiple of it is a peak-response estimate for that spectrum rather than a structural capability. No universal quasi-static qualification level for CubeSats exists to replace it: the CubeSat Design Specification \cite{cds} defers test levels to the launch provider, so they vary by vehicle and mission. \textbf{The defensible statement is therefore narrow: the rated design acceleration is 10.1~$g$ for 162~ms, and whether any specific satellite tolerates it depends on that satellite's own qualified load environment and on an integration review that has not been performed.} No payload has been mechanically qualified against this machine, and the payload-family accelerations in Table~\ref{tab:family}---which run to 14.0~$g$ at PocketQube---carry the same caveat. The atmosphere model is static, so absolute lifetimes carry severalfold uncertainty; an earlier version of this paper offered the invariance of the lifetime \emph{ratio} as the defensible result in their place, and Sec.~V-B records why that was wrong---the sweep supporting it could not have detected a change. The ratio is now quoted at a stated activity level and carries the same uncertainty as everything else derived from a static atmosphere. The thrust constant is winding-resolved in two dimensions; three-dimensional end effects of a few percent remain for finite-element closure. The brake law is first-order plate drag and remains an unverified arrest approximation. Conjunction figures, although refined at candidate minima, do not replace per-shot collision-avoidance products. Host budgets are parametric wherever stage properties are not public. The 9.445~kg sled input comes from historical Gen3 CAD solid volumes, whereas other dry-mass lines are modeled or assumed. This is not a mass extraction from a complete Gen5 assembly. The former 4.86~kg parametric sled estimate disagrees with those volumes. The CAD-derived value is adopted here and sets the headline velocity, but it is the as-drawn, unpocketed geometry---the structural analysis reports a 17-fold stress margin on the chassis plate, so a rib-stiffened redesign would recover mass, and none has been evaluated. The sensitivity, mechanical, thermal, power-electronics, and reliability analyses added here (Secs.~\ref{sec:sens}--\ref{sec:conops}) address the principal engineering questions only at analysis level. Several remain feasibility-significant. The commercial capacitor strings examined do not meet the ESR requirement; a flywheel motor-generator has since been shown to clear it at 35~m$\Omega$ against the 68~m$\Omega$ ceiling, needing no architecture change, but at mass parity rather than saving. The three-dimensional depth correction has been applied to the analytic field model and the result was unfavourable: resolving the field across the array's 90~mm depth, rather than sampling it at the centre plane and multiplying, reduces $K_t$ by \textbf{4.42\%} to 10.54~N per kA/m and $v_\mathrm{exit}$ to 16.03~m/s. That correction was computed on 2026-08-10 and held for three days rather than applied, because moving a frozen baseline is not a verification run's decision to take; it was adopted under change control on 2026-08-13, on the grounds that depth-resolving the integral is a change to the physics rather than a pasted correction factor and that collapsing the depth quadrature to a single plane reproduces the superseded value exactly, which keeps the ratio auditable. \textbf{Every figure in this paper is quoted after it.} The three-dimensional solve run the same day supports the underlying field model---a reduced-scalar-potential magnetostatic PDE on a 315{,}370-node tetrahedral mesh agrees with it to \textbf{0.059\%} at midgap---but it tests the field, not the depth average, and no three-dimensional check of the integrated thrust constant itself exists. The earlier 11.03~N per kA/m and 16.39~m/s appear here only where a superseded value is named as such. And hardware validation has not begun.

\section{Conclusion}
Gen5 is a fixed, system-level computational design for a magazine-fed linear-motor deployer. In the stated model it ejects an unmodified 3U CubeSat at 16.0~m/s and 10.1~$g$, with 18.8\% net electrical-to-payload efficiency and 0.0274~m/s simulated dispersion; the dry mass rollup is 126.6~kg. The orbital model predicts a 28.8~km semi-major-axis increase and a 1.60 lifetime multiplier at mean solar activity for one prograde shot. The commanded release profile is the proposed capability. The model also fails its mass-per-satellite criterion, and its side-fed reference CAD assembly has a measured track--cassette clash. It has no measured launch, payload qualification, selected host or completed integration review. The result is an analysed design with explicit limits, not a flight-qualified product. A matched mission comparison and physical tests are needed before claiming deployment or economic advantage.

\section{Future work: Gen6 scale-up}\label{sec:future}
Gen5 remains the sole design and performance baseline reported here. Gen6 is the proposed follow-on research programme: extend individually commanded orbital release toward a 1~km/s-class exit velocity and, if justified by mission analysis, beyond. This is a target rather than a selected configuration or a demonstrated result. At constant acceleration, 1~km/s requires about 2.0~km of stroke at 25~$g$ or 0.51~km at 100~$g$ from rest. Achieving it within Gen5's 1.3~m acceleration stroke would require approximately $3.9\times10^4$~$g$ on an ideal constant-acceleration estimate. The payload class and qualified load limit, guide geometry, energy and heat rejection, recoil and host control authority, containment, release interface and mission value must therefore be re-established together. Earlier gas-guide and independent spring-cell studies are unselected comparisons, not validated Gen6 hardware. The next evidence gates are a specified payload and host, a complete installed-system trade, an independently checked dynamics model and calibrated hardware tests. None of those gates is closed by the Gen5 calculations.
